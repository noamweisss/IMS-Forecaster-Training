Cloud Base and Top Forecasting

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:
  • Estimate convective cloud base from the surface T−Td spread, and state when the rule is valid
  • Use the morning sounding (CCL) to anticipate the afternoon convective base
  • Explain how stratiform bases lower with approaching fronts, rain and the marine inversion
  • Determine cloud top altitudes from satellite IR and radar echo tops, with their limitations
  • Read cloud base and top out of real METAR/SPECI reports
  • Apply local topographical knowledge to sub-grid cloud formation

Determining Cloud Base Height (CBH)

Cloud base height is a critical parameter for terminal operations and flight planning. The mechanism for determining CBH differs between convective and stratiform (layered) clouds.

Convective Clouds (Cumulus/TCU/CB)

For clouds triggered by surface heating, the base sits at the convective condensation level. The source course's working rule comes straight from the surface temperature (T) and dew point (Td):

Convective Base Rule
Base (thousands of ft) ≈ (T − Td) / 3, with T and Td in °C — i.e. the spread closes at about 3 °C per 1,000 ft as the rising parcel cools dry-adiabatically while its dew point falls more slowly. (A common international variant uses ~400 ft per °C, i.e. ÷2.5; use whichever your office verifies against.)

The rule is only reliable under two conditions, both from the source: (1) the convection starts from the ground, and (2) it is late morning to afternoon, after the nocturnal inversion has been destroyed by heating. To get ahead of the day, read the convective condensation level (CCL) off the morning sounding: it projects the base the afternoon's heating will produce. One caution — if the rising thermals punch into a very dry layer aloft, cloud may struggle to form at all despite a "favourable" surface spread.

Stratiform Clouds (Stratus/Altostratus)

Layered clouds are typically driven by moisture advection or large-scale lifting (e.g., warm fronts). Their base is determined by the level at which a moist layer becomes saturated. In coastal regions like Israel, the base of the marine inversion often acts as a trap for moisture, defining the Stratus base.

A stratiform base is a moisture story, not a heating story, and three patterns recur. A warm front is a "factory" for layered cloud: as it approaches with moisture aloft, the deck thickens and its base lowers — the passage of an upper-level ridge often heralds more high cloud and a falling base. Rain lowers the base too: precipitation saturates the sub-cloud air and ragged "scud" forms beneath the main deck. And a low stratus deck sits at the base of the marine inversion, which caps the moist layer.

Worked Example — an overnight base in the METARs (LLBG)
SPECI LLBG 120310Z 15007KT 5000 BR BKN018 23/20 → METAR 120350Z 16007KT 5000 BR SCT020 23/20 → METAR 120450Z 18007KT 7000 SCT020 25/21.
Read forward in time: the early-hours broken deck at 1,800 ft with mist (BR) and a 3°C spread (23/20) lifts and thins to scattered at 2,000 ft, and by 0450Z visibility has recovered to 7,000 m as morning heating widens the spread to 25/21. This is the textbook post-sunrise improvement of a moisture-trapped coastal base.

Determining Cloud Top Height

Knowing the vertical extent of a cloud layer allows pilots to determine if they can fly "over the top" or if they will remain in IMC (Instrument Meteorological Conditions) throughout a climb or descent.

Satellite IR Estimation

Infrared (IR) satellite sensors measure the brightness temperature of the cloud top. Since temperature typically decreases with height in the troposphere, we can map this temperature back to a specific altitude using a recent sounding (tephigram).

The conversion is a direct read-off the sounding. On a representative profile the source course gives anchor points of about −35°C ≈ 30,000 ft and +2°C ≈ 5,000 ft, so a −50°C anvil is higher still — but the exact mapping shifts with the local lapse rate, which is the third reason to use a recent, regional sounding rather than a fixed table.

IR Limitations
  • Thin Cirrus: Higher-level thin clouds let radiation from warmer layers below pass through, so the IR sensor reads a temperature that is too warm and underestimates the top height.
  • Partial Coverage: A pixel only partly filled by cloud (not 8/8) returns a temperature averaged between the cloud top and the surface.
  • Regional Lapse Rates: The temperature→height conversion differs from one air mass to another; a single fixed table will mislead.

Radar Echo Tops

Weather radar provides a more direct measurement of the vertical extent of precipitation-bearing clouds. The "Echo Top" product indicates the highest altitude at which a specific reflectivity threshold (usually 18 dBZ) is detected.

Worked Example — reading base and top from a convective SPECI (LLSD)
SPECI LLSD 161038Z 23020G30KT 8000 TS SHRA SCT015CB BKN030 17/15 Q1015=
Sde Dov: wind 230° at 20 kt gusting 30 (gust-front signature), 8 km visibility in a thunderstorm with rain showers, scattered CB based at 1,500 ft (SCT015CB) under a broken layer at 3,000 ft, and a near-saturated 17/15. The cloud groups give the base directly; the CB top itself is what you would then estimate from radar echo tops or the satellite IR, since the report only states the bases.

Local Effects in Israel

Topography plays a major role in local cloud forecasting:

  • Orographic Lifting: Westerly winds hitting the Judean or Galilean hills can force air to its LCL, creating low cloud bases and "mountain fog" even when coastal bases are higher.
  • Marine Inversion: During summer, a strong subsidence inversion traps moisture in a shallow layer. The height of this inversion directly controls whether the coastal plain experiences clear skies or a dense deck of Stratus.

Key Takeaways

  • Convective base ≈ (T − Td)/3 in thousands of feet — valid only for ground-based, late-morning-to-afternoon convection; the morning sounding's CCL projects the afternoon base.
  • Stratiform bases follow moisture: they lower as a warm front/upper ridge approaches, lower further in rain (scud), and sit at the marine-inversion base in coastal stratus.
  • Cloud tops come from satellite IR brightness temperature mapped through a recent sounding, or from radar echo tops (≈18 dBZ).
  • IR top estimates fail on thin cirrus, partially filled pixels, and across differing regional lapse rates — always use a local sounding.
  • The cloud groups in a METAR/SPECI give the base directly (e.g., SCT015CB = 1,500 ft); read base and top out of real reports.
  • Local topography modifies bases via orographic lifting and the marine inversion height.
