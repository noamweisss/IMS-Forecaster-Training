Visibility Restrictions: Fog and Haze

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:
  • Classify types of fog (radiation, advection, upslope) by formation mechanism
  • Identify the critical meteorological factors for fog onset and dissipation
  • Assess the impact of aerosol concentration and humidity on haze development
  • Interpret RVR (Runway Visual Range) trends in low-visibility conditions

Measuring Visibility

Visibility is defined as the greatest distance at which an object can be seen and recognized against a background. In aviation, we distinguish between general visibility (reported in METARs) and Runway Visual Range (RVR), which is the distance a pilot can see runway markings or lights from the center line.

Technical Standard
Modern airports use transmissometers or forward-scatter meters to measure the Meteorological Optical Range (MOR). RVR is then calculated using Allard's Law, accounting for ambient light and runway lamp intensity.

Fog: Formation and Classification

Fog is technically a cloud that is in contact with the ground, reducing horizontal visibility to less than 1,000 meters. For a forecaster, identifying the cooling mechanism is key to predicting its onset.

Radiation Fog

Formed by the radiative cooling of the earth's surface at night. It is most common in inland valleys and low-lying areas during high-pressure conditions.

  • Prerequisites: High surface humidity, clear skies, and light winds (3–5 knots).
  • The "Goldilocks" Wind: If the wind is calm, only dew forms. If the wind exceeds 5–10 knots, the air mixes with drier air aloft, dissipating the fog or lifting it into a Stratus layer.

Advection Fog

Occurs when warm, moist air moves over a colder surface (land or water). Unlike radiation fog, it can form with higher wind speeds (5–15 knots) and persist during the day.

Regional Context
In Israel, advection fog is common along the Mediterranean coast when moist marine air moves over colder coastal land during late winter and spring.

Other Fog Types

  • Upslope Fog: Stable air forced up a mountain slope, cooling adiabatically to its dewpoint.
  • Steam Fog (Sea Smoke): Cold air moving over warm water; evaporation from the water saturates the cold air above.
  • Frontal Fog: Warm rain falling through cold air ahead of a warm front, saturating the air via evaporation.

Visibility vs. Relative Humidity

Visibility is not only limited by liquid water (fog/mist) but also by dry particles (haze). The relationship between visibility and Relative Humidity (RH) is non-linear due to hygroscopic nuclei.

Condition Relative Humidity Aerosol Behavior
Dry Haze < 70% Particles remain small and dry.
Wet Haze 70% – 90% Hygroscopic particles begin to swell (deliquescence).
Mist 90% – 100% Droplets grow large enough to scatter visible light significantly.
Fog ~100% Droplets grow into a dense suspension; visibility < 1,000m.

Haze and Dust in Israel

Forecasting visibility in Israel requires monitoring synoptic sources of mineral dust from the Sahara and Arabian deserts. Key setups include:

  1. Sharav Lows: Deep North African depressions that transport dust ahead of the cold front.
  2. Red Sea Troughs: Associated with easterly winds that bring dust from the Jordanian/Saudi deserts.
  3. Cold Frontal Passage: Strong post-frontal winds can lift local dust (haboobs) in the Negev.
Operational Tool
Use satellite Dust RGB imagery to track the progression of dust plumes across the Mediterranean. These plumes can significantly reduce RVR even in the absence of cloud or fog.

Key Takeaways

  • Fog is defined as visibility < 1,000m; Mist is 1,000m to 5,000m.
  • Radiation fog requires clear skies, high humidity, and light (3-5 kt) winds.
  • Advection fog is more persistent and can occur with stronger winds.
  • Hygroscopic particles cause visibility to drop sharply as RH exceeds 70-80%.
  • Dust and haze in Israel are synoptically driven by Sharav lows and Red Sea Troughs.
