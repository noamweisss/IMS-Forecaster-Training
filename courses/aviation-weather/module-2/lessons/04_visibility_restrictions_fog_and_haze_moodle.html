Visibility Restrictions: Fog and Haze

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:
  • Distinguish visibility, MOR and RVR, and how transmissometers and forward-scatter meters measure them
  • Classify types of fog (radiation, advection, upslope) by formation mechanism
  • Identify the critical meteorological factors for fog onset and dissipation, including surface vs elevated inversions
  • Assess the impact of aerosol concentration and humidity on haze development
  • Classify the synoptic dust patterns affecting Israel (Red Sea Trough, Sharav low, winter low)
  • Interpret real low-visibility METAR/SPECI and RVR groups in operational decisions

Measuring Visibility

Visibility is defined as the greatest distance at which an object can be seen and recognized against a background. In aviation, we distinguish between general visibility (reported in METARs) and Runway Visual Range (RVR), which is the distance a pilot can see runway markings or lights from the center line.

Technical Standard
Modern airports use transmissometers or forward-scatter meters to measure the Meteorological Optical Range (MOR). RVR is then calculated using Allard's Law, accounting for ambient light and runway lamp intensity.

The two instruments work differently. A transmissometer sends a narrow beam (typically 550 nm, mid-visible) over a fixed baseline and measures how much is lost — the extinction coefficient — from which MOR follows (MOR is the distance over which a beam falls to 5% of its original intensity). A forward-scatter meter instead measures the light scattered from a small sample volume at an angle of 20–50°. Both are objective and high-resolution, but both sample only about a metre of air, and neither can tell why visibility is low — so a forward-scatter reading must be paired with humidity and precipitation sensors to separate fog from haze from rain.

Runway Visual Range (RVR) is the operational quantity: the distance along the centreline over which a pilot can see the runway markings, edge lights or centreline lights. It was originally assessed by counting edge lights (spaced 60 m apart — a CAT I runway needs at least 14 lamps visible), and is now computed from the instruments via Allard's law, which folds in the runway-light intensity and the background luminance (so the same fog gives a higher RVR at night against the lit lamps than against a bright day sky). RVR is measured at the touchdown, mid-point and rollout sections, reported in METARs and passed by ATC so the crew can judge whether an approach is legal.

Worked Example — decoding a fog SPECI
SPECI LLBG 152235Z VRB01KT 0050 R12/0175D R30/0125N FG VV001 20/18 Q1016 NOSIG=
Ben Gurion, special report: wind variable at 1 kt; prevailing visibility 50 m; RVR on runway 12 175 m and decreasing (D), on runway 30 125 m, no change (N); fog; sky obscured with a vertical visibility of 100 ft (VV001); temperature and dew point 20/18 — only 2°C apart, i.e. nearly saturated; QNH 1016; no significant change expected. This is a CAT-limited or below-minima situation, and the dewpoint depression of 2°C is exactly why.

Fog: Formation and Classification

Fog is technically a cloud that is in contact with the ground, reducing horizontal visibility to less than 1,000 meters. For a forecaster, identifying the cooling mechanism is key to predicting its onset.

Radiation Fog

Formed by the radiative cooling of the earth's surface at night. It is most common in inland valleys and low-lying areas during high-pressure conditions.

  • Prerequisites: High surface humidity, clear skies, and light winds (3–5 knots).
  • The "Goldilocks" Wind: If the wind is calm, only dew forms. If the wind exceeds 5–10 knots, the air mixes with drier air aloft, dissipating the fog or lifting it into a Stratus layer.

Advection Fog

Occurs when warm, moist air moves over a colder surface (land or water). Unlike radiation fog, it can form with higher wind speeds (5–15 knots) and persist during the day.

Regional Context
In Israel, advection fog is common along the Mediterranean coast when moist marine air moves over colder coastal land during late winter and spring.

Other Fog Types

  • Upslope Fog: Stable air forced up a mountain slope, cooling adiabatically to its dewpoint.
  • Steam Fog (Sea Smoke): Cold air moving over warm water; evaporation from the water saturates the cold air above.
  • Frontal Fog: Warm rain falling through cold air ahead of a warm front, saturating the air via evaporation.

Reading the Inversion (Israeli Fog)

Most Israeli fogs are radiation fogs, favoured in weak marine flow under a marine inversion — and the height of that inversion tells you where the fog forms and how its trend will read on the night's observations:

  • Surface inversion — saturation begins at the ground and the fog builds upward from there.
  • Elevated inversion (~0.5 km) — saturation first occurs at the inversion base, forming stratus (St) that then sinks, lowering through the night until it reaches the surface as fog.

Visibility vs. Relative Humidity

Visibility is not only limited by liquid water (fog/mist) but also by dry particles (haze). The relationship between visibility and Relative Humidity (RH) is non-linear due to hygroscopic nuclei.

Condition Relative Humidity Aerosol Behavior
Dry Haze < 70% Particles remain small and dry.
Wet Haze 70% – 90% Hygroscopic particles begin to swell (deliquescence).
Mist 90% – 100% Droplets grow large enough to scatter visible light significantly.
Fog ~100% Droplets grow into a dense suspension; visibility < 1,000m.

Haze and Dust in Israel

Forecasting visibility in Israel requires monitoring synoptic sources of mineral dust from the Sahara and Arabian deserts. Key setups include:

  1. Sharav Lows: Deep North African depressions that transport dust ahead of the cold front.
  2. Red Sea Troughs: Associated with easterly winds that bring dust from the Jordanian/Saudi deserts.
  3. Cold Frontal Passage: Strong post-frontal winds can lift local dust (haboobs) in the Negev.
Operational Tool
Use satellite Dust RGB imagery to track the progression of dust plumes across the Mediterranean. These plumes can significantly reduce RVR even in the absence of cloud or fog.

It helps to think in terms of source direction and a small set of recurring synoptic types. Dust arrives from the west (Sinai and North Africa) ahead of a cold low/cold front or in a warm (Sharav) low, and from the east with a Red Sea Trough or a Siberian ridge; strong frontal winds can also raise dust locally. Eliezer Ganor's classification of Israeli dust-storm synoptics is the standard shorthand:

ClassSynoptic typeTypical dust behaviour
DRed Sea TroughEasterly transport from the Arabian/Jordanian deserts.
ASharav (warm) lowPre-frontal dust ahead of a North African depression.
G / G1Winter low (Cyprus / North African cyclone)Frontal and post-frontal dust with strong winds.
CHigh / ridgeSubsidence; slow clearance, lingering haze.

Knowing how dust clears is just as operational: it leaves by sedimentation (gradual, largest particles first), by advection (a wind shift flushing it out), or — fastest — by rain washing it out. Dedicated dust models (IMS and regional centres) help, but they verify poorly, so the satellite Dust RGB and surface reports remain the backbone of the nowcast.

Worked Example — dust vs fog in the METAR
METAR LLET 270350Z 02006KT 8000 NSC 25/01 Q1008= then ...270450Z ... 6000 ...
Eilat: visibility falling from 8,000 m to 6,000 m with no significant cloud, in a Red Sea Trough. Temperature/dew point 25/01 — a dewpoint depression of 24°C, i.e. extremely dry. That alone tells you the obscuration is dust, not fog: fog needs near-saturation (recall the LLBG SPECI at 20/18), whereas dust drops visibility in bone-dry air. The humidity field is your fastest discriminator.

Key Takeaways

  • Fog is visibility < 1,000 m; mist/haze is 1,000–5,000 m. MOR is where a beam falls to 5% intensity; RVR (via Allard's law) is the operational centreline range.
  • Transmissometers (beam loss) and forward-scatter meters (scattered light) measure objectively but sample ~1 m and can't name the cause — pair them with humidity/precip sensors.
  • Radiation fog needs clear skies, high humidity and light (3–5 kt) winds; the inversion height sets whether it builds ground-up (surface) or sinks as lowering St (elevated).
  • Advection fog is more persistent and tolerates stronger winds; hygroscopic particles make visibility fall sharply once RH passes ~70–80%.
  • Israeli dust follows recurring synoptics (Ganor: Red Sea Trough D, Sharav low A, winter low G, high C) and clears by sedimentation, advection or — fastest — rain.
  • The dewpoint depression is your fastest discriminator: near-saturation = fog (LLBG 20/18); bone-dry with falling visibility = dust (LLET 25/01).
