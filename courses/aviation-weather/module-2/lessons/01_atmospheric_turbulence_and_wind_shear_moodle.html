By the end of this lesson you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between mechanical, convective, and clear-air turbulence (CAT)
  • Apply the standard turbulence-intensity scale (light through extreme) when interpreting and issuing reports
  • Identify synoptic and mesoscale features associated with severe mountain waves and CAT on the 300 hPa chart
  • Explain the formation and hazards of wake vortices
  • Diagnose gap (channelled) winds and why the strongest wind is found at the gap exit
  • Forecast low-level wind shear and microburst conditions during takeoff and landing

Introduction to Aviation Turbulence

Turbulence is one of the most significant weather-related hazards to aviation, accounting for thousands of minor injuries and substantial structural risk every year. For a forecaster, the challenge lies in identifying the specific atmospheric mechanisms—ranging from large-scale mountain waves to micro-scale wake vortices—that generate these chaotic air movements.

Operational Impact
While light turbulence is an inconvenience, severe turbulence involves large, abrupt changes in altitude and attitude, potentially leading to temporary loss of aircraft control and structural damage.

Types and Mechanisms

Aviation turbulence is generally classified by its underlying physical cause. Understanding these categories allows forecasters to look for specific triggers in synoptic charts and high-resolution models. Before the mechanisms, fix the language: turbulence reports use a standard intensity scale, and "moderate" to a forecaster must mean the same as "moderate" to the crew that reported it.

Intensity Effect on the aircraft
LightBrief, slight, erratic changes in altitude and/or attitude.
Light chopSlight, rapid, rhythmic bumpiness without noticeable change in altitude or attitude.
ModerateChanges in altitude/attitude and variations in indicated airspeed, but the aircraft remains in positive control.

Mechanical Turbulence

Mechanical turbulence occurs when strong winds are disrupted by surface features such as mountains, hills, or even large buildings. The severity is determined by wind speed, terrain roughness, and atmospheric stability. When winds exceeding 15–25 knots interact with large obstacles, turbulent eddies can extend as high as 5,000–10,000 feet.

Convective Turbulence

Convective turbulence is buoyancy-driven. When daytime heating makes the environmental lapse rate in the lowest kilometre steeper than the dry adiabatic rate, a displaced parcel stays warmer than its surroundings and keeps rising. The resulting thermals give the bumpy ride familiar on a hot afternoon, and they are strongest over surfaces that heat unevenly — a sun-baked apron next to irrigated fields, for instance.

The severe form arrives once those currents organise into cumulus, towering cumulus or cumulonimbus. Inside the cloud the air is unstable with strong up- and down-draughts; aircraft can exit far above or below their entry level. Crucially, the turbulence is not confined to the cloud — it is also found outside, especially above and below a CB, and in the spreading downdraughts beneath it.

Forecasting Tip
Convective turbulence has a strong diurnal signal over land, peaking in the afternoon with maximum surface heating and instability. Treat the clear air immediately above and below a mature CB as turbulent even when the cloud itself is being circumnavigated.

Clear Air Turbulence (CAT)

CAT is high-level turbulence (usually above 15,000 ft) occurring in cloud-free regions, making it invisible to pilots and standard radar. It is predominantly associated with the jet stream, specifically in regions of intense horizontal and vertical wind shear near the jet core.

Forecasting Tip
Approximately 60% of CAT reports are located near jet streams. Look for sharpening upper-level troughs and ridges, as well as regions with "transverse bands" in jet cirrus clouds on satellite imagery.

CAT has a characteristic geometry: outbreaks are typically hundreds of kilometres across horizontally but only hundreds of metres deep, and an individual patch lasts a few hours before shifting. That thinness is why a small altitude change often escapes it. On the 300 hPa chart, the favoured locations are the strong-shear flanks of the jet — classically on the cold (poleward) side and just below the core — together with sharp or sharpening troughs and ridges, and col areas where winds are weak but the direction changes markedly. Mountainous terrain enhances all of these.

Case Study — severe CAT out of Denver, December 1992
A DC-8 freighter climbing westbound out of Denver hit severe clear-air turbulence so violent it tore off an engine and roughly six metres of wing. The crew nevertheless landed safely. The lesson for the forecaster: CAT gives no visual or radar warning, so the diagnosis lives entirely in the upper-wind analysis and the shear it reveals.

Mountain Waves and Rotors

When stable air is forced over a mountain range, it can trigger gravity waves that propagate both vertically and far downwind. These "mountain waves" are capable of producing extreme turbulence.

  • Cap Clouds: Formed over the ridge as air is forced upward.
  • Lenticular Clouds: Stationed at the crests of the downstream waves.
  • Rotors: Violent roll eddies that form in the low levels beneath the wave crests, often marked by "roll clouds."
Case Study — BOAC 707 over Mount Fuji, 5 March 1966
In excellent visibility, a Boeing 707 encountered severe mountain-wave turbulence in the lee of Mount Fuji and broke up in flight. The setup was textbook: a very strong pressure gradient driving powerful W–NW winds across the mountain. The accident is a standing reminder that clear skies are no guarantee of smooth air when strong flow crosses high terrain — the absence of cloud can hide a violent wave field.

Wake Turbulence

Every aircraft producing lift generates a pair of counter-rotating wake vortices trailing from its wingtips. These vortices sink and slowly decay, but can remain hazardous for several minutes, especially in calm conditions.

Factor Effect on Wake Strength
Aircraft Weight Heavier aircraft produce stronger vortices.
Speed Strength is greatest at slow speeds (takeoff/landing).
Configuration "Clean" configurations (flaps up) produce more concentrated vortices.
Wind Strong winds dissipate vortices; calm winds allow them to linger over the runway.

Low-Level Wind Shear (LLWS) and Microbursts

Wind shear is defined as a change in wind speed and/or direction over a short distance. While it can occur at any altitude, Low-Level Wind Shear (below 2,000 ft AGL) is particularly dangerous during the approach and departure phases of flight.

The Microburst Hazard

A microburst is a concentrated, intense downdraft from a convective cloud that hits the ground and spreads out horizontally. An aircraft flying through a microburst first encounters a strong headwind (increasing lift), followed by a severe downdraft, and finally a strong tailwind (drastic loss of lift), which can be fatal at low altitudes.

Safety Alert
Aircraft are most vulnerable to microbursts when close to the ground and operating near stall speeds. Recognition of Virga (precipitation evaporating before reaching the ground) is a key visual indicator of potential dry microbursts.

Gap (Channelled) Winds

A second, terrain-driven form of low-level shear matters a great deal in a country shaped by wadis and rift valleys. Gap winds are low-level winds forced through a topographic constriction — a wadi, valley or strait. They can run from hundreds of metres to hundreds of kilometres and, in extreme cases, exceed 50 kt, with sharp shear along and across the "stream" that produces severe low-level turbulence. For military and low-level operations the practical figure is a wind that can swing between roughly 20 and 60 kt over a short distance.

The intuitive explanation — a "Venturi tube" in which Bernoulli's principle accelerates the air to its maximum at the narrowest point — is the wrong mental model, and getting it wrong puts the forecast in the wrong place. In reality the strongest wind appears at the exit of the gap, not at the throat.

Two things explain the exit maximum. First, the atmosphere has no rigid lid, so the simple tube analogy breaks down: at the exit the terrain widens horizontally and the flow is compressed vertically, dropping the pressure and setting up a gradient that accelerates the outflow. Second, low-level flow is not geostrophic — near the ground the three controls are the pressure gradient, the Coriolis force and friction from the terrain, and friction turns the wind to blow down the gradient from high to low rather than parallel to the isobars. A synoptic high on one side of the gap and a low on the other adds directly to the exit jet.

Forecasting Gap Winds
Stability matters: an unstable column (cold over warm) flows more smoothly and closer to geostrophic, while a stable layer (cold under warm) increases the effective friction and sharpens the channelling. A Bernoulli estimate relating the acceleration to the along-gap pressure difference is the simplest first guess, but it over-predicts the wind because it ignores friction — both surface friction and the drag of the gap walls. Slope gaps and superimposed mountain waves deflect the flow downward and accelerate it further.

Key Takeaways

  • Turbulence is categorized as mechanical, convective, CAT, or wake-induced, and reported on a fixed light-to-extreme intensity scale.
  • Convective turbulence peaks with afternoon heating and extends above and below a CB, not just inside it.
  • CAT is invisible and jet-stream driven (thin but wide); diagnose it from 300 hPa shear, sharp troughs/ridges and cols.
  • Mountain waves create vertical hazards, while rotors produce severe low-level turbulence — and clear skies are no guarantee of smooth air (Mt Fuji, 1966).
  • Wake vortices are strongest from heavy, slow aircraft in clean configurations.
  • Gap winds peak at the exit (not the throat); low-level flow is non-geostrophic, so it blows down the gradient and over-predicts on a frictionless Bernoulli estimate.
  • Microbursts present a unique threat by inducing a rapid transition from headwind to tailwind near the ground.
