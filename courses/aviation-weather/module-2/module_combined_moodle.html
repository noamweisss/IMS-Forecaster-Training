Introduction to Aviation Turbulence

Turbulence is one of the most significant weather-related hazards to aviation, accounting for thousands of minor injuries and substantial structural risk every year. For a forecaster, the challenge lies in identifying the specific atmospheric mechanisms—ranging from large-scale mountain waves to micro-scale wake vortices—that generate these chaotic air movements.

Operational Impact
While light turbulence is an inconvenience, severe turbulence involves large, abrupt changes in altitude and attitude, potentially leading to temporary loss of aircraft control and structural damage.

Types and Mechanisms

Aviation turbulence is generally classified by its underlying physical cause. Understanding these categories allows forecasters to look for specific triggers in synoptic charts and high-resolution models. Before the mechanisms, fix the language: turbulence reports use a standard intensity scale, and "moderate" to a forecaster must mean the same as "moderate" to the crew that reported it.

Intensity Effect on the aircraft
LightBrief, slight, erratic changes in altitude and/or attitude.
Light chopSlight, rapid, rhythmic bumpiness without noticeable change in altitude or attitude.
ModerateChanges in altitude/attitude and variations in indicated airspeed, but the aircraft remains in positive control.
Moderate chopRapid bumps and jolts without appreciable changes in altitude or attitude.
SevereLarge, abrupt changes in altitude/attitude with large airspeed variations; aircraft momentarily out of control.
ExtremeAircraft violently tossed and practically impossible to control; may cause structural damage.

Mechanical Turbulence

Mechanical turbulence occurs when strong winds are disrupted by surface features such as mountains, hills, or even large buildings. The severity is determined by wind speed, terrain roughness, and atmospheric stability. When winds exceeding 15–25 knots interact with large obstacles, turbulent eddies can extend as high as 5,000–10,000 feet.

Convective Turbulence

Convective turbulence is buoyancy-driven. When daytime heating makes the environmental lapse rate in the lowest kilometre steeper than the dry adiabatic rate, a displaced parcel stays warmer than its surroundings and keeps rising. The resulting thermals give the bumpy ride familiar on a hot afternoon, and they are strongest over surfaces that heat unevenly — a sun-baked apron next to irrigated fields, for instance.

The severe form arrives once those currents organise into cumulus, towering cumulus or cumulonimbus. Inside the cloud the air is unstable with strong up- and down-draughts; aircraft can exit far above or below their entry level. Crucially, the turbulence is not confined to the cloud — it is also found outside, especially above and below a CB, and in the spreading downdraughts beneath it.

Forecasting Tip
Convective turbulence has a strong diurnal signal over land, peaking in the afternoon with maximum surface heating and instability. Treat the clear air immediately above and below a mature CB as turbulent even when the cloud itself is being circumnavigated.

Clear Air Turbulence (CAT)

CAT is high-level turbulence (usually above 15,000 ft) occurring in cloud-free regions, making it invisible to pilots and standard radar. It is predominantly associated with the jet stream, specifically in regions of intense horizontal and vertical wind shear near the jet core.

Forecasting Tip
Approximately 60% of CAT reports are located near jet streams. Look for sharpening upper-level troughs and ridges, as well as regions with "transverse bands" in jet cirrus clouds on satellite imagery.

CAT has a characteristic geometry: outbreaks are typically hundreds of kilometres across horizontally but only hundreds of metres deep, and an individual patch lasts a few hours before shifting. That thinness is why a small altitude change often escapes it. On the 300 hPa chart, the favoured locations are the strong-shear flanks of the jet — classically on the cold (poleward) side and just below the core — together with sharp or sharpening troughs and ridges, and col areas where winds are weak but the direction changes markedly. Mountainous terrain enhances all of these.

Case Study — severe CAT out of Denver, December 1992
A DC-8 freighter climbing westbound out of Denver hit severe clear-air turbulence so violent it tore off an engine and roughly six metres of wing. The crew nevertheless landed safely. The lesson for the forecaster: CAT gives no visual or radar warning, so the diagnosis lives entirely in the upper-wind analysis and the shear it reveals.

Mountain Waves and Rotors

When stable air is forced over a mountain range, it can trigger gravity waves that propagate both vertically and far downwind. These "mountain waves" are capable of producing extreme turbulence.

  • Cap Clouds: Formed over the ridge as air is forced upward.
  • Lenticular Clouds: Stationed at the crests of the downstream waves.
  • Rotors: Violent roll eddies that form in the low levels beneath the wave crests, often marked by "roll clouds."
Case Study — BOAC 707 over Mount Fuji, 5 March 1966
In excellent visibility, a Boeing 707 encountered severe mountain-wave turbulence in the lee of Mount Fuji and broke up in flight. The setup was textbook: a very strong pressure gradient driving powerful W–NW winds across the mountain. The accident is a standing reminder that clear skies are no guarantee of smooth air when strong flow crosses high terrain — the absence of cloud can hide a violent wave field.

Wake Turbulence

Every aircraft producing lift generates a pair of counter-rotating wake vortices trailing from its wingtips. These vortices sink and slowly decay, but can remain hazardous for several minutes, especially in calm conditions.

Factor Effect on Wake Strength
Aircraft Weight Heavier aircraft produce stronger vortices.
Speed Strength is greatest at slow speeds (takeoff/landing).
Configuration "Clean" configurations (flaps up) produce more concentrated vortices.
Wind Strong winds dissipate vortices; calm winds allow them to linger over the runway.

Low-Level Wind Shear (LLWS) and Microbursts

Wind shear is defined as a change in wind speed and/or direction over a short distance. While it can occur at any altitude, Low-Level Wind Shear (below 2,000 ft AGL) is particularly dangerous during the approach and departure phases of flight.

The Microburst Hazard

A microburst is a concentrated, intense downdraft from a convective cloud that hits the ground and spreads out horizontally. An aircraft flying through a microburst first encounters a strong headwind (increasing lift), followed by a severe downdraft, and finally a strong tailwind (drastic loss of lift), which can be fatal at low altitudes.

Safety Alert
Aircraft are most vulnerable to microbursts when close to the ground and operating near stall speeds. Recognition of Virga (precipitation evaporating before reaching the ground) is a key visual indicator of potential dry microbursts.

Gap (Channelled) Winds

A second, terrain-driven form of low-level shear matters a great deal in a country shaped by wadis and rift valleys. Gap winds are low-level winds forced through a topographic constriction — a wadi, valley or strait. They can run from hundreds of metres to hundreds of kilometres and, in extreme cases, exceed 50 kt, with sharp shear along and across the "stream" that produces severe low-level turbulence. For military and low-level operations the practical figure is a wind that can swing between roughly 20 and 60 kt over a short distance.

The intuitive explanation — a "Venturi tube" in which Bernoulli's principle accelerates the air to its maximum at the narrowest point — is the wrong mental model, and getting it wrong puts the forecast in the wrong place. In reality the strongest wind appears at the exit of the gap, not at the throat.

Two things explain the exit maximum. First, the atmosphere has no rigid lid, so the simple tube analogy breaks down: at the exit the terrain widens horizontally and the flow is compressed vertically, dropping the pressure and setting up a gradient that accelerates the outflow. Second, low-level flow is not geostrophic — near the ground the three controls are the pressure gradient, the Coriolis force and friction from the terrain, and friction turns the wind to blow down the gradient from high to low rather than parallel to the isobars. A synoptic high on one side of the gap and a low on the other adds directly to the exit jet.

Forecasting Gap Winds
Stability matters: an unstable column (cold over warm) flows more smoothly and closer to geostrophic, while a stable layer (cold under warm) increases the effective friction and sharpens the channelling. A Bernoulli estimate relating the acceleration to the along-gap pressure difference is the simplest first guess, but it over-predicts the wind because it ignores friction — both surface friction and the drag of the gap walls. Slope gaps and superimposed mountain waves deflect the flow downward and accelerate it further.

Key Takeaways

  • Turbulence is categorized as mechanical, convective, CAT, or wake-induced, and reported on a fixed light-to-extreme intensity scale.
  • Convective turbulence peaks with afternoon heating and extends above and below a CB, not just inside it.
  • CAT is invisible and jet-stream driven (thin but wide); diagnose it from 300 hPa shear, sharp troughs/ridges and cols.
  • Mountain waves create vertical hazards, while rotors produce severe low-level turbulence — and clear skies are no guarantee of smooth air (Mt Fuji, 1966).
  • Wake vortices are strongest from heavy, slow aircraft in clean configurations.
  • Gap winds peak at the exit (not the throat); low-level flow is non-geostrophic, so it blows down the gradient and over-predicts on a frictionless Bernoulli estimate.
  • Microbursts present a unique threat by inducing a rapid transition from headwind to tailwind near the ground.

The Physics of In-flight Icing

Aircraft icing occurs when supercooled liquid water (SLW) droplets—water that remains in liquid form despite being below 0°C—impact the leading edges of an aircraft. These droplets freeze upon contact, disrupting the aerodynamic profile, increasing weight, and potentially causing engine failure or instrument malfunction.

Key Concept: SLW
Supercooled Liquid Water is most common in the temperature range 0°C to -15°C. Below -20°C, droplets are more likely to freeze into ice crystals spontaneously, reducing the icing risk.

The damage is not only structural. Ice on the leading edges spoils the aerofoil: lift falls, drag rises, and stall speed increases — so an iced wing can stall at a normal approach speed and attitude. Added weight and asymmetric shedding compound the problem, and control-surface and antenna icing affect handling. Just as dangerous is the loss of sensing: a blocked pitot or static port gives the crew unreliable airspeed and altitude, and iced engine inlets or probes can mislead the autoflight system. Several determinants set how bad it gets — droplet size, the cloud's liquid water content, the cloud type, and temperature — which is why the same outside-air temperature can produce trace icing in one cloud and severe icing in the next.

Types of Structural Icing

The type of ice that forms depends on the droplet size, the liquid water content (LWC) of the cloud, and the temperature of both the air and the aircraft skin.

Ice Type Appearance Formation Conditions Aviation Hazard
Rime Ice Milky, opaque, brittle Small drops, low LWC, temperatures below -10°C Disrupts airflow; easier to remove with boots.
Glaze (Clear) Ice Transparent, hard, glassy Large drops (SLD), high LWC, temperatures 0°C to -10°C Heavy and difficult to remove; spreads aft of protected areas.
Mixed Ice Irregular, rough Varied drop sizes and fluctuating temperatures Combines weight and aerodynamic penalties.

Freezing Rain: The SLD Threat

Freezing rain (FZRA) represents one of the most severe icing hazards. It typically occurs in warm frontal structures where snow falls through a "warm nose" (temperature > 0°C), melts into rain, and then falls back into a sub-freezing layer near the surface.

Severe Hazard
Freezing rain consists of Supercooled Large Droplets (SLD). Because of their mass, they do not freeze instantly upon impact but run back along the wing before freezing, creating a thick layer of clear ice in areas not protected by de-icing equipment.

Operational Diagnosis

Tephigram Analysis

To identify icing potential from a vertical sounding (Tephigram or Skew-T), forecasters use the following criteria:

  1. Identify the Temperature Range: Focus on layers between 0°C and -20°C.
  2. Check Saturation: Look for a dew point depression of < 5°C (indicating high humidity/cloud).
  3. Assess Stability: Convective clouds (unstable) typically contain higher liquid water content and thus present a higher risk of moderate to severe icing.

WAFS Icing Products

World Area Forecast System (WAFS) products provide global grids of icing potential. These are calculated based on fractional cloud cover and temperature. The fields are produced for five main flight levels (FL100, FL140, FL180, FL240, FL300).

Product Limitation
WAFS icing products represent potential based on large-scale model fields. They may miss localized severe icing in small-scale convective cells or complex terrain. Always cross-reference with PIREPs (Pilot Reports).

Beyond the Airframe: Engine, Pitot and Carburettor Icing

Ice that never touches the wing can still be fatal. A blocked pitot or static port deprives the crew — and the autoflight system — of reliable airspeed and altitude, and high-altitude convection can carry enough supercooled water and ice crystals to defeat even heated probes.

Case Study — Air France 447, 1 June 2009
En route from Rio de Janeiro to Paris, AF447 flew into a cluster of equatorial thunderstorms at cruise. The pitot probes iced over; the resulting inconsistent airspeed readings disengaged the autopilot, and subsequent manual mishandling led to an aerodynamic stall all the way to the ocean, with the loss of all 228 on board. The meteorological takeaway: convective avoidance is an icing concern at altitude, not only a turbulence one.

Piston aircraft add a counter-intuitive hazard. In the carburettor, the pressure drop and fuel evaporation cool the induction air sharply, so ice can form in the carburettor at outside air temperatures well above 0°C — typically in humid air anywhere from about +10°C to +25°C. It quietly throttles the engine; carburettor heat is the cure. The forecasting point is blunt: icing risk does not stop at the freezing level.

Protection, and Why It Can Fail

Aircraft carry anti-ice systems that prevent formation (heated leading edges, probes and windscreens) and de-ice systems that shed accumulated ice (pneumatic boots). Both protect the leading edges — and that is exactly their weakness. When supercooled large droplets or freezing rain run back and freeze aft of the protected surfaces, the boots and heaters cannot reach the ice, so protection is largely ineffective against runback icing.

On the Ground
Frozen contamination must be removed before take-off: even frost no thicker than sandpaper measurably degrades lift. De-icing/anti-icing fluids buy a limited "holdover time" before they fail — and that time is governed by the precipitation type and intensity you forecast, which makes the surface forecast a direct flight-safety input.

Key Takeaways

  • Icing is caused by Supercooled Liquid Water (SLW, mainly 0°C to −15°C) impacting aircraft surfaces; type and severity depend on droplet size, LWC, cloud type and temperature.
  • Icing spoils the aerofoil (lift down, drag up, stall speed up) and corrupts air data — a blocked pitot/static gives unreliable speed and altitude.
  • Glaze ice is the most hazardous structural type, often associated with SLD and high LWC; freezing rain (FZRA) forms when rain falls into a sub-freezing layer.
  • Run the tephigram decision tree: saturation in the 0 to −15°C layer, then convective vs deep-NS cloud, sets the severity category.
  • Engine, pitot and carburettor icing extend the threat beyond the airframe — carburettor icing occurs even above 0°C (AF447 is the cautionary case).
  • Leading-edge protection fails against runback ice; on the ground, all frozen contamination must be removed and holdover time tracks the precipitation forecast.
  • WAFS products provide a global baseline for icing risk but require local refinement against PIREPs.

  • Predict secondary hazards such as lightning, hail, squall lines and dry/wet microbursts

The Cumulonimbus (CB) Life Cycle

Cumulonimbus clouds are the most visually dramatic and operationally dangerous cloud type. A single convective cell typically progresses through three distinct stages over a 30 to 60-minute period.

1. Developing (Cumulus) Stage

Characterized exclusively by updrafts. Continuous rising air increases the cloud's vertical extent and internal turbulence. No precipitation or lightning occurs at this stage.

2. Mature Stage

The most dangerous phase. Precipitation begins to fall, dragging cold air down and creating downdrafts alongside the continuing updrafts. This stage features the highest turbulence, heavy rain, hail, and lightning.

3. Dissipating Stage

Downdrafts dominate the cell, "cutting off" the supply of warm, moist updrafts. The cloud begins to evaporate or flatten into an anvil (incus) at the tropopause. Precipitation weakens and eventually stops.

Hazards Associated with Convection

While lightning and heavy rain are common, several secondary hazards pose extreme risks to flight operations. A single mature CB packs almost the entire hazard catalogue into one cloud, which is why it is the common trigger behind so many of your products.

  • Hail: Formed in intense updrafts that cycle water droplets above the freezing level multiple times. Hail can cause catastrophic structural damage to engines and windshields.
  • Lightning: Results from charge separation between ice crystals and water droplets. Can disrupt electronics and blind pilots temporarily.
  • Gust Fronts: The leading edge of cold downdrafts spreading out at the surface. They cause sharp wind shifts and can trigger new convective cells.
  • Squall Lines: Linear arrangements of CB cells that act as a formidable barrier to air traffic.

Because the CB bundles icing, turbulence, lightning, hail and wind shear together, it is the phenomenon behind a large share of aerodrome warnings, AIRMETs and SIGMETs. When you issue one, the spatial coverage descriptor matters as much as the hazard: isolated (individual cells), occasional (well-separated), frequent (little or no separation), and embedded (hidden in other cloud or haze) each imply a very different avoidance problem for the crew.

Forecaster Note
In Israel, squall lines are particularly common ahead of active cold fronts. They are notoriously difficult to navigate and often require complete avoidance by pilots.
Dry vs Wet Microbursts
The convective downdraft can reach the surface as a microburst. A wet microburst arrives with heavy rain; a dry microburst falls from a high-based cloud whose rain evaporates before reaching the ground — so the giveaway is virga and, sometimes, ragged rings of blown dust rather than visible rain. Both are detectable on Doppler radar as a low-level divergence couplet. Do not dismiss innocuous-looking high-based convection.
Precursor — Altocumulus Castellanus
Turreted mid-level cloud (altocumulus castellanus) signals instability already present at mid-levels. It is an early warning that the afternoon's deeper convection has fuel waiting aloft — a sign not to underestimate.

Estimating the Cloud Top

How high a CB reaches decides whether it can be over-flown at all, and the fastest estimate comes from the satellite. The cloud-top infrared brightness temperature gives the temperature of the top; matching that temperature against the day's sounding (or the model temperature profile) converts it to a height. Colder top means a taller, more vigorous storm; an overshooting top — colder still and punching above the anvil — is a reliable marker of a severe updraft. A sonde or the model's vertical structure cross-checks the satellite estimate.

Worked Example — top from IR
An anvil shows an IR brightness temperature of about −58°C. Reading −58°C off the day's sounding places the top near the tropopause, on the order of FL380 for a typical profile — a storm no airliner will be topping. A colder reading means a still-taller cell, and a localised cold spot above the anvil flags an overshooting top and a severe updraft. This is how a single satellite image becomes an overflight decision.

Stability Indices and Prediction

Forecasters use several thermodynamic indices derived from soundings to evaluate convective potential. No single index is definitive; they must be used in combination.

Index Meaning Threshold for Severe Wx
CAPE Convective Available Potential Energy (buoyancy) > 1000 J/kg
LI Lifted Index (T_environment - T_parcel at 500hPa) < -4
CIN Convective Inhibition (energy needed to trigger) High values prevent storm onset
Thermodynamic Forcing
Convection requires three ingredients: Moisture, Instability, and a Lifting Mechanism (e.g., a front, topography, or surface convergence).

Convective Triggers in Israel

The triad is universal; what changes locally is the lift. In Israel the recurring triggers are worth knowing by name, because they tell you where and when to expect initiation:

  • Sea-breeze convergence — the marine breeze pushing inland, especially on an easterly background gradient, converging with the prevailing flow; often active from the early hours along the coast.
  • Pre-frontal gust fronts — ahead of an advancing cold front, the classic squall-line setup.
  • Low-level convergence lines — a secondary but reliable focus when combined with afternoon heating.
  • Orographic lift — over the central mountains and the northern Golan.
  • Synoptic patterns — a western trough or cold front; lingering troughiness after the peak of a winter system; and an active Red Sea Trough.
Why Convection Is Hard to Forecast
Convection resists both timing and placement. Nowcasting struggles to catch initiation, and models routinely mis-locate cells, so the forecast leans on mesoscale signals — sea-breeze timing, sounding instability, and the evolving cumulus field on satellite — backed by continuous nowcasting rather than a single model run.

Key Takeaways

  • The CB life cycle consists of the Developing, Mature, and Dissipating stages; the Mature stage is the peak hazard period.
  • A single CB bundles icing, turbulence, lightning, hail and shear — the common trigger behind aerodrome warnings, AIRMETs and SIGMETs (mind the coverage descriptor: isolated/occasional/frequent/embedded).
  • Dry microbursts fall from high-based cloud (virga, dust rings) and are as dangerous as wet ones; altocumulus castellanus is an early instability cue.
  • Estimate the CB top from the satellite IR brightness temperature matched to the sounding; an overshooting top flags a severe updraft.
  • Forecasting relies on the moisture–instability–lift triad and stability indices (CAPE, LI), but timing and placement remain hard — nowcasting and mesoscale signals carry the load.
  • In Israel, watch the sea-breeze convergence, pre-frontal gust fronts, orographic lift over the central mountains/Golan, and active Red Sea Trough setups.

Measuring Visibility

Visibility is defined as the greatest distance at which an object can be seen and recognized against a background. In aviation, we distinguish between general visibility (reported in METARs) and Runway Visual Range (RVR), which is the distance a pilot can see runway markings or lights from the center line.

Technical Standard
Modern airports use transmissometers or forward-scatter meters to measure the Meteorological Optical Range (MOR). RVR is then calculated using Allard's Law, accounting for ambient light and runway lamp intensity.

The two instruments work differently. A transmissometer sends a narrow beam (typically 550 nm, mid-visible) over a fixed baseline and measures how much is lost — the extinction coefficient — from which MOR follows (MOR is the distance over which a beam falls to 5% of its original intensity). A forward-scatter meter instead measures the light scattered from a small sample volume at an angle of 20–50°. Both are objective and high-resolution, but both sample only about a metre of air, and neither can tell why visibility is low — so a forward-scatter reading must be paired with humidity and precipitation sensors to separate fog from haze from rain.

Runway Visual Range (RVR) is the operational quantity: the distance along the centreline over which a pilot can see the runway markings, edge lights or centreline lights. It was originally assessed by counting edge lights (spaced 60 m apart — a CAT I runway needs at least 14 lamps visible), and is now computed from the instruments via Allard's law, which folds in the runway-light intensity and the background luminance (so the same fog gives a higher RVR at night against the lit lamps than against a bright day sky). RVR is measured at the touchdown, mid-point and rollout sections, reported in METARs and passed by ATC so the crew can judge whether an approach is legal.

Worked Example — decoding a fog SPECI
SPECI LLBG 152235Z VRB01KT 0050 R12/0175D R30/0125N FG VV001 20/18 Q1016 NOSIG=
Ben Gurion, special report: wind variable at 1 kt; prevailing visibility 50 m; RVR on runway 12 175 m and decreasing (D), on runway 30 125 m, no change (N); fog; sky obscured with a vertical visibility of 100 ft (VV001); temperature and dew point 20/18 — only 2°C apart, i.e. nearly saturated; QNH 1016; no significant change expected. This is a CAT-limited or below-minima situation, and the dewpoint depression of 2°C is exactly why.

Fog: Formation and Classification

Fog is technically a cloud that is in contact with the ground, reducing horizontal visibility to less than 1,000 meters. For a forecaster, identifying the cooling mechanism is key to predicting its onset.

Radiation Fog

Formed by the radiative cooling of the earth's surface at night. It is most common in inland valleys and low-lying areas during high-pressure conditions.

  • Prerequisites: High surface humidity, clear skies, and light winds (3–5 knots).
  • The "Goldilocks" Wind: If the wind is calm, only dew forms. If the wind exceeds 5–10 knots, the air mixes with drier air aloft, dissipating the fog or lifting it into a Stratus layer.

Advection Fog

Occurs when warm, moist air moves over a colder surface (land or water). Unlike radiation fog, it can form with higher wind speeds (5–15 knots) and persist during the day.

Regional Context
In Israel, advection fog is common along the Mediterranean coast when moist marine air moves over colder coastal land during late winter and spring.

Other Fog Types

  • Upslope Fog: Stable air forced up a mountain slope, cooling adiabatically to its dewpoint.
  • Steam Fog (Sea Smoke): Cold air moving over warm water; evaporation from the water saturates the cold air above.
  • Frontal Fog: Warm rain falling through cold air ahead of a warm front, saturating the air via evaporation.

Reading the Inversion (Israeli Fog)

Most Israeli fogs are radiation fogs, favoured in weak marine flow under a marine inversion — and the height of that inversion tells you where the fog forms and how its trend will read on the night's observations:

  • Surface inversion — saturation begins at the ground and the fog builds upward from there.
  • Elevated inversion (~0.5 km) — saturation first occurs at the inversion base, forming stratus (St) that then sinks, lowering through the night until it reaches the surface as fog.

Visibility vs. Relative Humidity

Visibility is not only limited by liquid water (fog/mist) but also by dry particles (haze). The relationship between visibility and Relative Humidity (RH) is non-linear due to hygroscopic nuclei.

Condition Relative Humidity Aerosol Behavior
Dry Haze < 70% Particles remain small and dry.
Wet Haze 70% – 90% Hygroscopic particles begin to swell (deliquescence).
Mist 90% – 100% Droplets grow large enough to scatter visible light significantly.
Fog ~100% Droplets grow into a dense suspension; visibility < 1,000m.

Haze and Dust in Israel

Forecasting visibility in Israel requires monitoring synoptic sources of mineral dust from the Sahara and Arabian deserts. Key setups include:

  1. Sharav Lows: Deep North African depressions that transport dust ahead of the cold front.
  2. Red Sea Troughs: Associated with easterly winds that bring dust from the Jordanian/Saudi deserts.
  3. Cold Frontal Passage: Strong post-frontal winds can lift local dust (haboobs) in the Negev.
Operational Tool
Use satellite Dust RGB imagery to track the progression of dust plumes across the Mediterranean. These plumes can significantly reduce RVR even in the absence of cloud or fog.

It helps to think in terms of source direction and a small set of recurring synoptic types. Dust arrives from the west (Sinai and North Africa) ahead of a cold low/cold front or in a warm (Sharav) low, and from the east with a Red Sea Trough or a Siberian ridge; strong frontal winds can also raise dust locally. Eliezer Ganor's classification of Israeli dust-storm synoptics is the standard shorthand:

ClassSynoptic typeTypical dust behaviour

ASharav (warm) lowPre-frontal dust ahead of a North African depression.
G / G1Winter low (Cyprus / North African cyclone)Frontal and post-frontal dust with strong winds.

Knowing how dust clears is just as operational: it leaves by sedimentation (gradual, largest particles first), by advection (a wind shift flushing it out), or — fastest — by rain washing it out. Dedicated dust models (IMS and regional centres) help, but they verify poorly, so the satellite Dust RGB and surface reports remain the backbone of the nowcast.

Worked Example — dust vs fog in the METAR
METAR LLET 270350Z 02006KT 8000 NSC 25/01 Q1008= then ...270450Z ... 6000 ...
Eilat: visibility falling from 8,000 m to 6,000 m with no significant cloud, in a Red Sea Trough. Temperature/dew point 25/01 — a dewpoint depression of 24°C, i.e. extremely dry. That alone tells you the obscuration is dust, not fog: fog needs near-saturation (recall the LLBG SPECI at 20/18), whereas dust drops visibility in bone-dry air. The humidity field is your fastest discriminator.

Key Takeaways

  • Fog is visibility < 1,000 m; mist/haze is 1,000–5,000 m. MOR is where a beam falls to 5% intensity; RVR (via Allard's law) is the operational centreline range.
  • Transmissometers (beam loss) and forward-scatter meters (scattered light) measure objectively but sample ~1 m and can't name the cause — pair them with humidity/precip sensors.
  • Radiation fog needs clear skies, high humidity and light (3–5 kt) winds; the inversion height sets whether it builds ground-up (surface) or sinks as lowering St (elevated).
  • Advection fog is more persistent and tolerates stronger winds; hygroscopic particles make visibility fall sharply once RH passes ~70–80%.
  • Israeli dust follows recurring synoptics (Ganor: Red Sea Trough D, Sharav low A, winter low G, high C) and clears by sedimentation, advection or — fastest — rain.
  • The dewpoint depression is your fastest discriminator: near-saturation = fog (LLBG 20/18); bone-dry with falling visibility = dust (LLET 25/01).

Cloud Base and Top Forecasting

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:
  • Estimate convective cloud base from the surface T−Td spread, and state when the rule is valid
  • Use the morning sounding (CCL) to anticipate the afternoon convective base
  • Explain how stratiform bases lower with approaching fronts, rain and the marine inversion
  • Determine cloud top altitudes from satellite IR and radar echo tops, with their limitations
  • Read cloud base and top out of real METAR/SPECI reports
  • Apply local topographical knowledge to sub-grid cloud formation

Determining Cloud Base Height (CBH)

Cloud base height is a critical parameter for terminal operations and flight planning. The mechanism for determining CBH differs between convective and stratiform (layered) clouds.

Convective Clouds (Cumulus/TCU/CB)

For clouds triggered by surface heating, the base sits at the convective condensation level. The source course's working rule comes straight from the surface temperature (T) and dew point (Td):

Convective Base Rule
Base (thousands of ft) ≈ (T − Td) / 3, with T and Td in °C — i.e. the spread closes at about 3 °C per 1,000 ft as the rising parcel cools dry-adiabatically while its dew point falls more slowly. (A common international variant uses ~400 ft per °C, i.e. ÷2.5; use whichever your office verifies against.)

The rule is only reliable under two conditions, both from the source: (1) the convection starts from the ground, and (2) it is late morning to afternoon, after the nocturnal inversion has been destroyed by heating. To get ahead of the day, read the convective condensation level (CCL) off the morning sounding: it projects the base the afternoon's heating will produce. One caution — if the rising thermals punch into a very dry layer aloft, cloud may struggle to form at all despite a "favourable" surface spread.

Stratiform Clouds (Stratus/Altostratus)

Layered clouds are typically driven by moisture advection or large-scale lifting (e.g., warm fronts). Their base is determined by the level at which a moist layer becomes saturated. In coastal regions like Israel, the base of the marine inversion often acts as a trap for moisture, defining the Stratus base.

A stratiform base is a moisture story, not a heating story, and three patterns recur. A warm front is a "factory" for layered cloud: as it approaches with moisture aloft, the deck thickens and its base lowers — the passage of an upper-level ridge often heralds more high cloud and a falling base. Rain lowers the base too: precipitation saturates the sub-cloud air and ragged "scud" forms beneath the main deck. And a low stratus deck sits at the base of the marine inversion, which caps the moist layer.

Worked Example — an overnight base in the METARs (LLBG)
SPECI LLBG 120310Z 15007KT 5000 BR BKN018 23/20 → METAR 120350Z 16007KT 5000 BR SCT020 23/20 → METAR 120450Z 18007KT 7000 SCT020 25/21.
Read forward in time: the early-hours broken deck at 1,800 ft with mist (BR) and a 3°C spread (23/20) lifts and thins to scattered at 2,000 ft, and by 0450Z visibility has recovered to 7,000 m as morning heating widens the spread to 25/21. This is the textbook post-sunrise improvement of a moisture-trapped coastal base.

Determining Cloud Top Height

Knowing the vertical extent of a cloud layer allows pilots to determine if they can fly "over the top" or if they will remain in IMC (Instrument Meteorological Conditions) throughout a climb or descent.

Satellite IR Estimation

Infrared (IR) satellite sensors measure the brightness temperature of the cloud top. Since temperature typically decreases with height in the troposphere, we can map this temperature back to a specific altitude using a recent sounding (tephigram).

The conversion is a direct read-off the sounding. On a representative profile the source course gives anchor points of about −35°C ≈ 30,000 ft and +2°C ≈ 5,000 ft, so a −50°C anvil is higher still — but the exact mapping shifts with the local lapse rate, which is the third reason to use a recent, regional sounding rather than a fixed table.

IR Limitations
  • Thin Cirrus: Higher-level thin clouds let radiation from warmer layers below pass through, so the IR sensor reads a temperature that is too warm and underestimates the top height.
  • Partial Coverage: A pixel only partly filled by cloud (not 8/8) returns a temperature averaged between the cloud top and the surface.
  • Regional Lapse Rates: The temperature→height conversion differs from one air mass to another; a single fixed table will mislead.

Radar Echo Tops

Weather radar provides a more direct measurement of the vertical extent of precipitation-bearing clouds. The "Echo Top" product indicates the highest altitude at which a specific reflectivity threshold (usually 18 dBZ) is detected.

Worked Example — reading base and top from a convective SPECI (LLSD)
SPECI LLSD 161038Z 23020G30KT 8000 TS SHRA SCT015CB BKN030 17/15 Q1015=
Sde Dov: wind 230° at 20 kt gusting 30 (gust-front signature), 8 km visibility in a thunderstorm with rain showers, scattered CB based at 1,500 ft (SCT015CB) under a broken layer at 3,000 ft, and a near-saturated 17/15. The cloud groups give the base directly; the CB top itself is what you would then estimate from radar echo tops or the satellite IR, since the report only states the bases.

Local Effects in Israel

Topography plays a major role in local cloud forecasting:

  • Orographic Lifting: Westerly winds hitting the Judean or Galilean hills can force air to its LCL, creating low cloud bases and "mountain fog" even when coastal bases are higher.
  • Marine Inversion: During summer, a strong subsidence inversion traps moisture in a shallow layer. The height of this inversion directly controls whether the coastal plain experiences clear skies or a dense deck of Stratus.

Key Takeaways

  • Convective base ≈ (T − Td)/3 in thousands of feet — valid only for ground-based, late-morning-to-afternoon convection; the morning sounding's CCL projects the afternoon base.
  • Stratiform bases follow moisture: they lower as a warm front/upper ridge approaches, lower further in rain (scud), and sit at the marine-inversion base in coastal stratus.
  • Cloud tops come from satellite IR brightness temperature mapped through a recent sounding, or from radar echo tops (≈18 dBZ).
  • IR top estimates fail on thin cirrus, partially filled pixels, and across differing regional lapse rates — always use a local sounding.
  • The cloud groups in a METAR/SPECI give the base directly (e.g., SCT015CB = 1,500 ft); read base and top out of real reports.
  • Local topography modifies bases via orographic lifting and the marine inversion height.
