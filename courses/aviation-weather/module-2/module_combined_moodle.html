By the end of this lesson you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between mechanical, convective, and clear-air turbulence (CAT)
  • Identify synoptic and mesoscale features associated with severe mountain waves
  • Explain the formation and hazards of wake vortices

Introduction to Aviation Turbulence

Turbulence is one of the most significant weather-related hazards to aviation, accounting for thousands of minor injuries and substantial structural risk every year. For a forecaster, the challenge lies in identifying the specific atmospheric mechanisms—ranging from large-scale mountain waves to micro-scale wake vortices—that generate these chaotic air movements.

Operational Impact
While light turbulence is an inconvenience, severe turbulence involves large, abrupt changes in altitude and attitude, potentially leading to temporary loss of aircraft control and structural damage.

Types and Mechanisms

Aviation turbulence is generally classified by its underlying physical cause. Understanding these categories allows forecasters to look for specific triggers in synoptic charts and high-resolution models.

Mechanical Turbulence

Mechanical turbulence occurs when strong winds are disrupted by surface features such as mountains, hills, or even large buildings. The severity is determined by wind speed, terrain roughness, and atmospheric stability. When winds exceeding 15–25 knots interact with large obstacles, turbulent eddies can extend as high as 5,000–10,000 feet.

Clear Air Turbulence (CAT)

CAT is high-level turbulence (usually above 15,000 ft) occurring in cloud-free regions, making it invisible to pilots and standard radar. It is predominantly associated with the jet stream, specifically in regions of intense horizontal and vertical wind shear near the jet core.

Forecasting Tip
Approximately 60% of CAT reports are located near jet streams. Look for sharpening upper-level troughs and ridges, as well as regions with "transverse bands" in jet cirrus clouds on satellite imagery.

Mountain Waves and Rotors

When stable air is forced over a mountain range, it can trigger gravity waves that propagate both vertically and far downwind. These "mountain waves" are capable of producing extreme turbulence.

  • Cap Clouds: Formed over the ridge as air is forced upward.
  • Lenticular Clouds: Stationed at the crests of the downstream waves.
  • Rotors: Violent roll eddies that form in the low levels beneath the wave crests, often marked by "roll clouds."

Wake Turbulence

Every aircraft producing lift generates a pair of counter-rotating wake vortices trailing from its wingtips. These vortices sink and slowly decay, but can remain hazardous for several minutes, especially in calm conditions.

Factor Effect on Wake Strength
Aircraft Weight Heavier aircraft produce stronger vortices.
Speed Strength is greatest at slow speeds (takeoff/landing).
Configuration "Clean" configurations (flaps up) produce more concentrated vortices.
Wind Strong winds dissipate vortices; calm winds allow them to linger over the runway.

Low-Level Wind Shear (LLWS) and Microbursts

Wind shear is defined as a change in wind speed and/or direction over a short distance. While it can occur at any altitude, Low-Level Wind Shear (below 2,000 ft AGL) is particularly dangerous during the approach and departure phases of flight.

The Microburst Hazard

A microburst is a concentrated, intense downdraft from a convective cloud that hits the ground and spreads out horizontally. An aircraft flying through a microburst first encounters a strong headwind (increasing lift), followed by a severe downdraft, and finally a strong tailwind (drastic loss of lift), which can be fatal at low altitudes.

Safety Alert
Aircraft are most vulnerable to microbursts when close to the ground and operating near stall speeds. Recognition of Virga (precipitation evaporating before reaching the ground) is a key visual indicator of potential dry microbursts.

Key Takeaways

  • Turbulence is categorized as mechanical, convective, CAT, or wake-induced.
  • CAT is invisible and jet-stream driven; look for high horizontal/vertical shear.
  • Mountain waves create vertical hazards, while rotors produce severe low-level turbulence.
  • Wake vortices are strongest from heavy, slow aircraft in clean configurations.
  • Microbursts present a unique threat by inducing a rapid transition from headwind to tailwind.

The Physics of In-flight Icing

Aircraft icing occurs when supercooled liquid water (SLW) droplets—water that remains in liquid form despite being below 0°C—impact the leading edges of an aircraft. These droplets freeze upon contact, disrupting the aerodynamic profile, increasing weight, and potentially causing engine failure or instrument malfunction.

Key Concept: SLW
Supercooled Liquid Water is most common in the temperature range 0°C to -15°C. Below -20°C, droplets are more likely to freeze into ice crystals spontaneously, reducing the icing risk.

Types of Structural Icing

The type of ice that forms depends on the droplet size, the liquid water content (LWC) of the cloud, and the temperature of both the air and the aircraft skin.

Ice Type Appearance Formation Conditions Aviation Hazard
Rime Ice Milky, opaque, brittle Small drops, low LWC, temperatures below -10°C Disrupts airflow; easier to remove with boots.
Glaze (Clear) Ice Transparent, hard, glassy Large drops (SLD), high LWC, temperatures 0°C to -10°C Heavy and difficult to remove; spreads aft of protected areas.
Mixed Ice Irregular, rough Varied drop sizes and fluctuating temperatures Combines weight and aerodynamic penalties.

Freezing Rain: The SLD Threat

Freezing rain (FZRA) represents one of the most severe icing hazards. It typically occurs in warm frontal structures where snow falls through a "warm nose" (temperature > 0°C), melts into rain, and then falls back into a sub-freezing layer near the surface.

Severe Hazard
Freezing rain consists of Supercooled Large Droplets (SLD). Because of their mass, they do not freeze instantly upon impact but run back along the wing before freezing, creating a thick layer of clear ice in areas not protected by de-icing equipment.

Operational Diagnosis

Tephigram Analysis

To identify icing potential from a vertical sounding (Tephigram or Skew-T), forecasters use the following criteria:

  1. Identify the Temperature Range: Focus on layers between 0°C and -20°C.
  2. Check Saturation: Look for a dew point depression of < 5°C (indicating high humidity/cloud).
  3. Assess Stability: Convective clouds (unstable) typically contain higher liquid water content and thus present a higher risk of moderate to severe icing.

WAFS Icing Products

World Area Forecast System (WAFS) products provide global grids of icing potential. These are calculated based on fractional cloud cover and temperature. The fields are produced for five main flight levels (FL100, FL140, FL180, FL240, FL300).

Product Limitation
WAFS icing products represent potential based on large-scale model fields. They may miss localized severe icing in small-scale convective cells or complex terrain. Always cross-reference with PIREPs (Pilot Reports).

Key Takeaways

  • Icing is caused by Supercooled Liquid Water (SLW) impacting aircraft surfaces.
  • Glaze ice is the most hazardous structural type, often associated with SLD and high LWC.
  • Freezing rain (FZRA) occurs when rain falls into a sub-freezing layer near the ground.
  • Sounding analysis focuses on saturated layers (dewpoint depression < 5°C) between 0°C and -20°C.
  • WAFS products provide a global baseline for icing risk but require local refinement.

Convective Hazards and CB Clouds

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:
  • Identify the life cycle stages of a Cumulonimbus (CB) cell
  • Describe the internal dynamics of severe convective updrafts and downdrafts
  • Predict secondary hazards such as lightning, hail, and squall lines
  • Evaluate convective potential using stability indices

The Cumulonimbus (CB) Life Cycle

Cumulonimbus clouds are the most visually dramatic and operationally dangerous cloud type. A single convective cell typically progresses through three distinct stages over a 30 to 60-minute period.

1. Developing (Cumulus) Stage

Characterized exclusively by updrafts. Continuous rising air increases the cloud's vertical extent and internal turbulence. No precipitation or lightning occurs at this stage.

2. Mature Stage

The most dangerous phase. Precipitation begins to fall, dragging cold air down and creating downdrafts alongside the continuing updrafts. This stage features the highest turbulence, heavy rain, hail, and lightning.

3. Dissipating Stage

Downdrafts dominate the cell, "cutting off" the supply of warm, moist updrafts. The cloud begins to evaporate or flatten into an anvil (incus) at the tropopause. Precipitation weakens and eventually stops.

Hazards Associated with Convection

While lightning and heavy rain are common, several secondary hazards pose extreme risks to flight operations.

  • Hail: Formed in intense updrafts that cycle water droplets above the freezing level multiple times. Hail can cause catastrophic structural damage to engines and windshields.
  • Lightning: Results from charge separation between ice crystals and water droplets. Can disrupt electronics and blind pilots temporarily.
  • Gust Fronts: The leading edge of cold downdrafts spreading out at the surface. They cause sharp wind shifts and can trigger new convective cells.
  • Squall Lines: Linear arrangements of CB cells that act as a formidable barrier to air traffic.
Forecaster Note
In Israel, squall lines are particularly common ahead of active cold fronts. They are notoriously difficult to navigate and often require complete avoidance by pilots.

Stability Indices and Prediction

Forecasters use several thermodynamic indices derived from soundings to evaluate convective potential. No single index is definitive; they must be used in combination.

Index Meaning Threshold for Severe Wx
CAPE Convective Available Potential Energy (buoyancy) > 1000 J/kg
LI Lifted Index (T_environment - T_parcel at 500hPa) < -4
CIN Convective Inhibition (energy needed to trigger) High values prevent storm onset
Thermodynamic Forcing
Convection requires three ingredients: Moisture, Instability, and a Lifting Mechanism (e.g., a front, topography, or surface convergence).

Key Takeaways

  • The CB life cycle consists of the Developing, Mature, and Dissipating stages.
  • The Mature stage is the peak hazard period, featuring simultaneous updrafts and downdrafts.
  • Secondary hazards like hail and gust fronts can extend several miles from the cloud core.
  • A Squall Line is a organized line of storms that typically requires avoidance.
  • Forecasting relies on stability indices (CAPE, LI) and identifying lifting mechanisms.

  • Identify the critical meteorological factors for fog onset and dissipation
  • Assess the impact of aerosol concentration and humidity on haze development

Measuring Visibility

Visibility is defined as the greatest distance at which an object can be seen and recognized against a background. In aviation, we distinguish between general visibility (reported in METARs) and Runway Visual Range (RVR), which is the distance a pilot can see runway markings or lights from the center line.

Technical Standard
Modern airports use transmissometers or forward-scatter meters to measure the Meteorological Optical Range (MOR). RVR is then calculated using Allard's Law, accounting for ambient light and runway lamp intensity.

Fog: Formation and Classification

Fog is technically a cloud that is in contact with the ground, reducing horizontal visibility to less than 1,000 meters. For a forecaster, identifying the cooling mechanism is key to predicting its onset.

Radiation Fog

Formed by the radiative cooling of the earth's surface at night. It is most common in inland valleys and low-lying areas during high-pressure conditions.

  • Prerequisites: High surface humidity, clear skies, and light winds (3–5 knots).
  • The "Goldilocks" Wind: If the wind is calm, only dew forms. If the wind exceeds 5–10 knots, the air mixes with drier air aloft, dissipating the fog or lifting it into a Stratus layer.

Advection Fog

Occurs when warm, moist air moves over a colder surface (land or water). Unlike radiation fog, it can form with higher wind speeds (5–15 knots) and persist during the day.

Regional Context
In Israel, advection fog is common along the Mediterranean coast when moist marine air moves over colder coastal land during late winter and spring.

Other Fog Types

  • Upslope Fog: Stable air forced up a mountain slope, cooling adiabatically to its dewpoint.
  • Steam Fog (Sea Smoke): Cold air moving over warm water; evaporation from the water saturates the cold air above.
  • Frontal Fog: Warm rain falling through cold air ahead of a warm front, saturating the air via evaporation.

Visibility vs. Relative Humidity

Visibility is not only limited by liquid water (fog/mist) but also by dry particles (haze). The relationship between visibility and Relative Humidity (RH) is non-linear due to hygroscopic nuclei.

Condition Relative Humidity Aerosol Behavior
Dry Haze < 70% Particles remain small and dry.
Wet Haze 70% – 90% Hygroscopic particles begin to swell (deliquescence).
Mist 90% – 100% Droplets grow large enough to scatter visible light significantly.
Fog ~100% Droplets grow into a dense suspension; visibility < 1,000m.

Haze and Dust in Israel

Forecasting visibility in Israel requires monitoring synoptic sources of mineral dust from the Sahara and Arabian deserts. Key setups include:

  1. Sharav Lows: Deep North African depressions that transport dust ahead of the cold front.
  2. Red Sea Troughs: Associated with easterly winds that bring dust from the Jordanian/Saudi deserts.
  3. Cold Frontal Passage: Strong post-frontal winds can lift local dust (haboobs) in the Negev.
Operational Tool
Use satellite Dust RGB imagery to track the progression of dust plumes across the Mediterranean. These plumes can significantly reduce RVR even in the absence of cloud or fog.

Key Takeaways

  • Fog is defined as visibility < 1,000m; Mist is 1,000m to 5,000m.
  • Radiation fog requires clear skies, high humidity, and light (3-5 kt) winds.
  • Advection fog is more persistent and can occur with stronger winds.
  • Hygroscopic particles cause visibility to drop sharply as RH exceeds 70-80%.
  • Dust and haze in Israel are synoptically driven by Sharav lows and Red Sea Troughs.

Determining Cloud Base Height (CBH)

Cloud base height is a critical parameter for terminal operations and flight planning. The mechanism for determining CBH differs between convective and stratiform (layered) clouds.

Convective Clouds (Cumulus/TCU/CB)

For clouds triggered by surface heating, the base is located at the Lifting Condensation Level (LCL). A simple rule of thumb for estimation is based on the surface temperature (T) and dew point (Td) in Celsius:

Formula
Base (ft) ≈ (T - Td) / 2.5 * 1000
Note: Some models use 3°C per 1,000ft for dry adiabatic cooling of the T-Td spread.

Stratiform Clouds (Stratus/Altostratus)

Layered clouds are typically driven by moisture advection or large-scale lifting (e.g., warm fronts). Their base is determined by the level at which a moist layer becomes saturated. In coastal regions like Israel, the base of the marine inversion often acts as a trap for moisture, defining the Stratus base.

Determining Cloud Top Height

Knowing the vertical extent of a cloud layer allows pilots to determine if they can fly "over the top" or if they will remain in IMC (Instrument Meteorological Conditions) throughout a climb or descent.

Satellite IR Estimation

Infrared (IR) satellite sensors measure the brightness temperature of the cloud top. Since temperature typically decreases with height in the troposphere, we can map this temperature back to a specific altitude using a recent sounding (tephigram).

IR Limitations
  • Thin Cirrus: Higher-level thin clouds allow radiation from warmer layers below to pass through, causing the IR sensor to underestimate the cloud top height.
  • Partial Coverage: If a pixel is only partially covered by cloud, the resulting temperature is an average of the cloud top and the ground.

Radar Echo Tops

Weather radar provides a more direct measurement of the vertical extent of precipitation-bearing clouds. The "Echo Top" product indicates the highest altitude at which a specific reflectivity threshold (usually 18 dBZ) is detected.

Local Effects in Israel

Topography plays a major role in local cloud forecasting:

  • Orographic Lifting: Westerly winds hitting the Judean or Galilean hills can force air to its LCL, creating low cloud bases and "mountain fog" even when coastal bases are higher.
  • Marine Inversion: During summer, a strong subsidence inversion traps moisture in a shallow layer. The height of this inversion directly controls whether the coastal plain experiences clear skies or a dense deck of Stratus.

Key Takeaways

  • Convective cloud bases can be estimated using surface T and Td spreads.
  • Stratiform bases are often tied to inversion heights or moisture advection layers.
  • Cloud tops are estimated via satellite IR brightness temperature or radar echo tops.
  • Satellite IR can be misleading for thin cirrus or sub-pixel cloud features.
  • Local topography significantly modifies cloud bases via orographic lifting.
