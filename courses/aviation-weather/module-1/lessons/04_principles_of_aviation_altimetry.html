Lesson 4: Principles of Aviation Altimetry

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Define and differentiate between QNH, QFE, and Standard Altimeter Settings.
  • Determine the appropriate transition altitude and transition level for flight operations.
  • Calculate true altitude based on non-standard temperature and pressure variations.

Aircraft altimeters are essentially aneroid barometers calibrated to read altitude rather than pressure. Because atmospheric pressure constantly changes with weather systems, altimeters must be continually adjusted to provide accurate vertical positioning. Failure to apply the correct altimeter setting can lead to controlled flight into terrain (CFIT) or loss of vertical separation between aircraft.

Meteorologists provide the pressure settings (like QNH) in METARs and ATIS broadcasts. Pilots dial these settings into the Kollsman window of their altimeter to ensure they are referencing the correct datum plane.

The Three Reference Planes: QNH, QFE, and QNE

Depending on the phase of flight, an aircraft's altimeter will reference one of three pressure datums. Understanding which setting is appropriate is a core competency for both pilots and aviation forecasters.

Setting Reference Plane Altimeter Reads... Primary Use Case
QNH Mean Sea Level (MSL) Altitude above sea level Takeoff, approach, and low-level terrain clearance.
QFE Airfield Elevation Height above the runway Final approach, circuit flying (reads zero on touchdown).
Standard 1013.25 hPa (29.92 inHg) Flight Level (FL) En-route flying to ensure safe vertical separation between all aircraft.

Transition Layer

To avoid a situation where two nearby aircraft are flying on different altimeter settings (risking a collision), air traffic procedures dictate a specific altitude where all aircraft switch to the Standard setting.

During climb, aircraft switch from QNH to Standard at the Transition Altitude (fixed for an airport). During descent, they switch from Standard back to the local QNH at the Transition Level (which varies based on current pressure). The gap between them is the Transition Layer.

Temperature Errors: "From High to Low, Look Out Below"

Altimeters are calibrated for the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA). If the actual air temperature is significantly colder than standard, the air column is denser and compressed. In this scenario, the altimeter will over-read; the aircraft is actually lower than the indicated altitude.

Cold Weather Corrections
During extreme cold weather operations, pilots must manually calculate and add altitude corrections to their published approach minimums to ensure they maintain adequate clearance over terrain and obstacles.

Key Takeaways

  • QNH provides Altitude above Mean Sea Level (used for terrain clearance).
  • QFE provides Height above the airfield (reads zero upon touchdown).
  • Standard Setting (1013.25 hPa) provides Flight Levels (used for en-route separation).
  • Aircraft transition to Standard setting during climb at the Transition Altitude, and to QNH during descent at the Transition Level.
  • Extremely cold temperatures cause altimeters to over-read, making the aircraft dangerously lower than indicated.
