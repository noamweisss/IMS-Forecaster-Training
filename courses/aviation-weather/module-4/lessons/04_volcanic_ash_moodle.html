Volcanic Ash

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:
  • Explain the specific hazards volcanic ash poses to jet aircraft operations.
  • Interpret Volcanic Ash Advisories (VAA) from VAACs.
  • Outline the role of atmospheric dispersion modeling for predicting ash plume trajectories.

The Hidden Hazard to Jet Aviation

Approximately 15 to 25 volcanoes erupt worldwide each year with sufficient force to eject volcanic ash into jet cruising levels (FL300 and above). Unlike meteorological clouds, volcanic ash plumes are often invisible to aircraft weather radar. Weather radar is tuned to detect large water droplets and ice crystals; dry volcanic ash particles are too small and lack the dielectric properties needed to reflect the radar beam back to the aircraft.

The hazards posed by an aircraft inadvertently flying into an ash plume are severe and potentially catastrophic:

  • Engine Stall: Volcanic ash contains silicates with a melting point lower than the operating temperatures of modern jet turbine engines. The ash melts inside the combustion chamber and resolidifies on the cooler turbine blades, choking off airflow and causing complete engine failure.
  • Abrasion: Ash is essentially pulverized rock and glass. It sandblasts the aircraft's outer frame, leading to expensive repairs, and violently pits the cockpit windows, blinding the flight crew.
  • System Contamination: Ash clogs pitot tubes (disrupting airspeed indications) and contaminates cabin air systems with toxic sulfur dioxide.
Historical Context
In June 1982, British Airways Flight 9 (a Boeing 747) encountered an unforecasted ash plume from Mount Galunggung over Indonesia. The aircraft lost power in all four engines and descended to 12,000 feet before the crew managed to restart some engines and make an emergency landing. This incident catalyzed the creation of the modern volcanic ash monitoring system.

Detection and Dispersion Modeling

Because aircraft radar is useless against ash, aviation relies entirely on external meteorological monitoring. This responsibility falls to nine Volcanic Ash Advisory Centres (VAACs) worldwide, such as the London VAAC hosted by the UK Met Office.

When an eruption occurs, the VAAC immediately utilizes satellite imagery to detect and pinpoint the ash plume. They then input the eruption details (plume height, duration, and source geometry) into an Atmospheric Dispersion Model.

For instance, the London VAAC uses the NAME (Numerical Atmospheric-dispersion Modelling Environment) model. The model calculates the 3D trajectory of the ash particles as they are carried by upper-level winds, factoring in particle settling and atmospheric diffusion over time.

Volcanic Ash Advisories (VAA)

The output of the dispersion model is translated into a Volcanic Ash Advisory (VAA). The VAA provides coordinate polygons mapping the current position of the ash cloud and its forecasted position at +6, +12, and +18 hours. These advisories are critical for airspace management and are often accompanied by graphical representations.

Meteorological Watch Offices (MWOs) use the VAA polygons directly to issue SIGMETs for their respective Flight Information Regions (FIRs). Unlike thunderstorm SIGMETs, volcanic ash SIGMETs are not discretionary—if an ash plume is forecast to enter an FIR, the corresponding SIGMET must be issued.

Key Takeaways

  • Volcanic ash is invisible to standard aircraft weather radar because it lacks water content.
  • Ash melts in jet engines causing stalls, and its abrasive nature destroys outer structures and windshields.
  • Nine VAACs monitor global volcanism and run complex atmospheric dispersion models (like NAME) to track and forecast ash plumes.
  • VAAs provide the critical polygon coordinates that MWOs use to issue mandatory airspace-closing SIGMETs.
