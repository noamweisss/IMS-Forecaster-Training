Tropical Storms

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Classify tropical cyclones using the Saffir-Simpson scale.
  • Interpret global warning center advisories and graphical track forecasts.

Formation and Structure

A tropical cyclone is a rapidly rotating storm system characterized by a low-pressure center, a closed low-level atmospheric circulation, strong winds, and a spiral arrangement of thunderstorms that produce heavy rain. Unlike mid-latitude cyclones, they lack weather fronts and are driven by latent heat release from condensing water vapor.

For a tropical storm to form and intensify, several specific atmospheric and oceanic conditions must align simultaneously:

  • Warm Ocean Waters: Sea surface temperatures (SST) of at least 26.5 °C through a depth of 50 meters to fuel the thermodynamic engine.
  • Coriolis Force: A location at least 5° latitude away from the equator. Without the Coriolis effect, the low-pressure center cannot maintain its cyclonic rotation.
  • Low Vertical Wind Shear: High wind shear tears the storm structure apart, preventing the necessary vertical stacking of the convection.
  • Atmospheric Instability and Moisture: Rapid cooling with height and high mid-tropospheric humidity.

Once formed, a mature storm exhibits three distinct structural regions: the Eye (a region of mostly calm weather and sinking air at the center), the Eyewall (a ring of towering thunderstorms containing the storm's most severe weather and highest winds), and the Spiral Rainbands (bands of convection spiraling outward).

The Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale (SSHS)

To communicate the hazard level, storms are classified by their maximum sustained winds. The widely used Saffir-Simpson scale categorizes hurricanes from 1 to 5 based on a 1-minute average sustained wind speed measured at 10 meters above the surface.

Category Sustained Winds (Knots) Expected Damage
Category 1 64 - 82 kt Some damage. Unsecured objects become projectiles.
Category 2 83 - 95 kt Extensive damage. Shallowly rooted trees snapped or uprooted.
Category 3 (Major) 96 - 112 kt Devastating damage. Electricity and water unavailable for days.
Category 4 (Major) 113 - 136 kt Catastrophic damage. Severe damage to well-built homes.
Category 5 (Major) > 136 kt Catastrophic damage. High percentage of framed homes destroyed.
Aviation Impact
While airports lock down and evacuate aircraft during landfalling Category 3+ storms, even a Category 1 storm or a strong Tropical Storm (34-63 kt) presents extreme hazards to aviation, including severe low-level wind shear, microbursts in the outer rainbands, and prolonged IFR/LIFR conditions.

Warning Centers and Track Forecasts

Global monitoring and forecasting of tropical cyclones is managed by a network of six Regional Specialized Meteorological Centres (RSMCs) and five Tropical Cyclone Warning Centres (TCWCs), designated by the WMO. For example, the National Hurricane Center (NHC) in Miami is the RSMC for the North Atlantic and Eastern Pacific.

These centers issue regular advisories and graphical track forecasts. A key component of these forecasts is the "Cone of Uncertainty."

Interpreting the Cone
A common misconception is that the cone represents the size of the storm. It does not. It only represents the historical forecast error of the storm's center. Impacts such as hurricane-force wind gusts and torrential rain can, and frequently do, occur far outside the cone.

In addition to deterministic tracks, advanced centers like the ECMWF provide Strike Probability Maps. These ensemble-based products calculate the probability that a tropical cyclone will pass within a specific radius (e.g., 120 km) of a given location over a set time window, offering a more nuanced view of risk than the cone alone.

Key Takeaways

  • Formation requires warm SSTs (> 26.5 °C), low wind shear, deep moisture, and sufficient Coriolis force.
  • The Saffir-Simpson scale classifies storms based on 1-minute sustained winds, with Category 3 and above considered Major Hurricanes.
  • RSMCs and TCWCs are the global authorities for tracking and issuing advisories on tropical cyclones.
  • The Cone of Uncertainty visualizes the probable track of the storm's center, but hazardous conditions routinely extend well beyond its boundaries.
