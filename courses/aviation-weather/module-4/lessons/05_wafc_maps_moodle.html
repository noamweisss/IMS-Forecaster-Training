WAFC Maps & Global Forecast Charts

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between High-level (SWH) and Medium-level (SWM) SIGWX charts.
  • Decode standard aviation meteorological parameters (jetstreams, CAT, CBs) on WAFC maps.
  • Differentiate between isolated, occasional, and frequent cumulonimbus coverage as defined by ICAO.

The World Area Forecast System (WAFS)

The World Area Forecast System (WAFS) is a global program developed by ICAO and the WMO to provide high-quality, consistent en-route meteorological guidance for international flights. It is operated by two World Area Forecast Centres (WAFCs): WAFC London and WAFC Washington.

WAFCs broadcast global wind, temperature, humidity, and Significant Weather (SIGWX) forecasts in two formats: traditional PNG charts for direct viewing, and BUFR format, which allows end-users to generate customized, localized charts using visualization software.

SWH vs. SWM Charts

SIGWX charts are split into two operational levels:

  • High-Level SIGWX (SWH): Valid for Flight Levels 250 to 630. This is a global dataset.
  • Medium-Level SIGWX (SWM): Valid for Flight Levels 100 to 450. Unlike SWH, SWM is not global. It is issued for specific regional areas (e.g., EUR, MEA, NAT). SWM charts include finer detail regarding in-cloud icing and turbulence that are not present on high-level charts.
Chart Overlap
Because SWH starts at FL250 and SWM goes up to FL450, there is an intentional overlap. Pilots flying in this middle band must consult the chart that best captures the specific weather hazards (like in-cloud icing on the SWM) relevant to their route.

Decoding the Charts

Both SWH and SWM charts use standard ICAO symbology to depict critical aviation hazards. You must be able to quickly decode these features:

  • Jetstreams: Depicted when the core wind speed is ≥ 80 knots. Indicates location, core speed, and altitude.
  • Clear Air Turbulence (CAT): Depicted by a dashed line enclosing the area. Labeled with intensity (Moderate, Severe) and the flight levels affected.
  • Tropopause Heights: Indicated by boxes showing the flight level of the tropopause, including localized "trop highs" and "trop lows."

Cumulonimbus (CB) Coverage Definitions

When forecasting thunderstorms (CBs) on a SIGWX chart, ICAO mandates specific terminology to describe the spatial coverage of the storm cells within the affected area. Scalloped lines denote the boundary of the convective area.

Coverage Terminology
  • ISOL (Isolated): Individual CBs with a maximum spatial coverage of less than 50% of the forecast area.
  • OCNL (Occasional): Well-separated CBs with a spatial coverage of 50% to 75% of the forecast area.
  • FRQ (Frequent): CBs with little or no separation, covering more than 75% of the forecast area.
  • EMBD (Embedded): CBs that are hidden within other cloud layers and cannot be readily recognized visually by pilots.

These terms dictate how an aircraft can navigate the hazard. An ISOL or OCNL area suggests that a pilot equipped with airborne weather radar can safely pick their way between the storms. A FRQ area implies a solid wall of thunderstorms that must be avoided entirely.

Key Takeaways

  • WAFCs distribute high-quality en-route weather forecasts globally to support international aviation.
  • SWH (High-level) charts are global and cover FL250 to FL630. SWM (Medium-level) charts are regional, cover FL100 to FL450, and include more detail on in-cloud icing.
  • Jetstreams are plotted when the core wind is 80 knots or greater.
  • CB coverage is strictly defined: ISOL (<50%), OCNL (50-75%), and FRQ (>75%).
