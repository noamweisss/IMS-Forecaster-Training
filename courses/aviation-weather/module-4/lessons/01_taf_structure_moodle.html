The Terminal Aerodrome Forecast (TAF)

The Terminal Aerodrome Forecast (TAF) is a concise statement of expected meteorological conditions significant to aviation at an airport. It is prepared by an Aerodrome Forecast Office (AFO) and is the primary tool used by airlines and pilots for flight planning and fuel load decisions.

The definition of the "terminal" area is strictly limited to the airspace within 8 km (5 SM) of the center of an airport's runway complex. Conditions outside this circle require different reporting mechanisms or careful use of proximity modifiers.

The Vicinity (VC) Modifier
Vicinity (VC) denotes a donut-shaped area between circles with radii of 5 SM and 10 SM from the center of the runway complex. It is used exclusively in combination with fog (FG), showers (SH), and thunderstorms (TS).

Including a VC modifier (e.g., VCSH, VCTS) informs the user about significant weather near the airfield without imposing the stringent operational impacts required by a PROB30 or TEMPO group. It acts as an advisory for the pilot to maintain situational awareness without grounding the flight or forcing alternate fuel requirements.

TAF Writing Philosophy

The modern paradigm in TAF writing is the user-friendly TAF. In the past, forecasters often wrote lengthy, un-amendable forecasts that included every possible variation in weather ("throwing in the kitchen sink"). This led to ambiguous products that penalized airline operations.

A high-quality TAF meets six criteria: it is Clear, Concise, Consistent, Relevant, Timely, and Accurate. Your goal as a forecaster is to enhance flight safety while saving customers time and money.

Avoid Over-Forecasting
Limit the use of PROB groups and conditional modifiers. Do not hedge—make a decision. If you cannot pinpoint exact numbers, at least ensure the trend and the flight category are correct. Aim to write a TAF that is no more than 5 lines long.

Aviation Flight Categories

Rather than obsessing over minute fluctuations in ceiling or visibility, structure your TAF around Aviation Flight Categories. The primary objective is to get the category correct (e.g., VFR vs. IFR) and spend most of your forecasting energy on the periods expected to fall below VFR.

Flight Category Ceiling Visibility Operational Impact
VFR (Visual Flight Rules) > 3000 ft > 5 SM (8000 m) Routine operations. Mention cirrus only if that is all there is to report.
MVFR (Marginal VFR) 1000 to 3000 ft 3 to 5 SM Increased caution; may impact general aviation.
IFR (Instrument Flight Rules) 500 to < 1000 ft 1 to < 3 SM Requires instrument approaches; significant delays possible.
LIFR (Low IFR) / CAT II/III < 500 ft < 1 SM Critical thresholds for the airport. Major operational restrictions.

Change and Probability Groups

Conditions during the validity period are expressed with a small, fixed set of change groups. Choosing the right one is half of writing a clean TAF:

GroupMeaningTypical use
FM (FMyyGGgg)A rapid, permanent change at a stated time; it supersedes everything before it.Frontal passage, sharp wind shift.
BECMG (yyGG/yyGG)A gradual permanent change completing within the window; the new condition prevails by the end.Slow onset or clearance.
TEMPO (yyGG/yyGG)Temporary fluctuations, each lasting under an hour and less than half the period in total; conditions return between.Showers, brief visibility drops.
PROB30 / PROB40A 30% / 40% probability of the stated conditions (PROB50+ is not used — make a decision instead).Significant but uncertain weather.
The BECMG trap
A BECMG window leaves the conditions ambiguous while it is open: a reader cannot tell exactly when within BECMG 2306/2310 the cloud actually lowers. Keep BECMG windows short, and when a change is genuinely sharp (a front, a sea-breeze onset) prefer FM at a single time. This is the most common source of an un-amendable, unhelpful TAF.
Worked Example — decoding a TAF
TAF LLBG 231100Z 2312/2412 30012KT 9999 FEW040 BECMG 2316/2318 32018G28KT TEMPO 2318/2322 4000 SHRA BKN020CB PROB30 2319/2321 30025G40KT=
Issued day 23 at 1100Z, valid 2312/2412 (24 h). Prevailing: wind 300°/12 kt, visibility ≥10 km, few cloud at 4,000 ft. BECMG 2316/2318: the wind gradually backs/strengthens to 320°/18 gusting 28 between 16 and 18Z. TEMPO 2318/2322: temporary drops to 4 km in rain showers with broken CB at 2,000 ft. PROB30 2319/2321: a 30% chance of 300°/25 gusting 40. Read as flight categories: VFR throughout, with a TEMPO dip toward MVFR/IFR in the showers — exactly the period a dispatcher will plan contingency fuel around.

Structuring the TAF

When drafting a TAF, establish the overarching trend and write to the aviation flight categories first. Ensure you add the most detail to the first 6 hours of the forecast, as this is the critical operational period for incoming flights.

Group similar visibilities and ceilings into coherent scenarios (scenario forecasting) rather than jumping sporadically between categories. For example, if you introduce a TEMPO group, try not to jump more than one category at a time (e.g., from VFR directly down to CAT II IFR).

Key Takeaways

  • A TAF covers the terminal area within 5 SM; the VC modifier covers the donut between 5 SM and 10 SM (used with TS, SH, FG).
  • Change groups: FM = instant permanent change; BECMG = gradual permanent change; TEMPO = temporary fluctuations that return; PROB30/40 = probability. Don't use PROB50+.
  • The BECMG window is ambiguous while open — keep it short, and use FM for sharp changes to keep the TAF amendable.
  • A user-friendly TAF is concise (ideally ≤ 5 lines), forecasts by flight category, and avoids hedging with excessive PROB.
  • Concentrate the highest detail and accuracy in the first 6 hours, the critical operational window.
